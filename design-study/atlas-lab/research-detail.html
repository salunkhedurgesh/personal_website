Research / Kinematic intelligence

One demonstrated skill.
Different robot bodies.

A robot can learn a motion from a demonstration. But what happens when its arm changes? Our work uses an analytical understanding of robot geometry to help a learned skill transfer.

Science Robotics · 2026 · Sthithpragya Gupta, Durgesh Haribhau Salunkhe & Aude Billard

Read the paper · PDF ↗Understand the idea ↓

The question

How much of a skill
belongs to the robot?

Imagine showing one robot how to move toward a target. A second robot might have different link lengths, joint orientations or joint limits. The same command can produce a different motion—or request a configuration it cannot reach.

Our approach brings these differences into the control policy itself. The robot’s kinematic structure becomes part of how a demonstrated behavior is represented and executed.

What we mean by “kinematic intelligence”

An internal representation of the robot’s joint limits, singularities and connectivity: which configurations are available, and how the robot can move between them. This gives learning an explicit description of the body it controls.

New to the topic?

Three words that help.

Configuration
The robot’s joint positions.
Singularity
A configuration where the relationship between joint motion and tool motion loses rank.
Policy
A rule mapping the robot’s state to its next action.

Method & evidence

Geometry enters
before execution.

A reading guide to the contribution. The paper contains the formal assumptions, derivations and experiments.

01 / Model

Classify the geometry.

The framework builds on an analytical classification of noncuspidal three-revolute-joint robots, useful building blocks for many commercial manipulators.

02 / Learn

Represent the behavior.

One or more demonstrations define a globally stable dynamical system. Robot-specific kinematic constraints enter the resulting joint-space policy.

03 / Transfer

Evaluate across bodies.

The study tests transfer on simulated and real robots with varied link geometries and joint configurations, including redundant and nonredundant robots.

What the experiments show

The paper reports execution of demonstrated skills across the tested robot structures without retuning. The contribution connects an analytical description of kinematics with learning from demonstration.

Scope matters

The analytical foundation concerns noncuspidal 3R structures. The paper’s conditions and experiments define the scope of its results; they are not a universal guarantee for arbitrary robots, tasks or environments.
