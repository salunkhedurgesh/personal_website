In France, operating rooms are already under pressure, and staffing shortages have made the need for robotic assistance immediate, not hypothetical.
This urgency is increasingly recognized by researchers, who are already demonstrating how robotic systems could support clinical teams directly in the operating room.


I want to develop safe, reliable and intuitive robotic systems that can meaningfully support clinicians in demanding care environments.

To make my vision more concrete, imagine a very simple moment in the operating room:
a surgeon says, “Please pass me the scalpel.”
This image looks simple.

But, any deviation, ambiguity, or execution failure can have catastrophic consequences, and so the robot’s behavior must remain dependable and intuitive for the clinician.

But for a robot, this apparently simple request hides three major scientific barriers.

the robot must move safely, predictably, and explainably in the real world.

The second barrier is skill scalability:
if we teach one robot a useful skill today, we should not have to start from zero when a new robot arrives tomorrow.

And the third barrier is embodied alignment:
the robot must act in a way that is physically grounded, understandable, and aligned with human intent.

This is why I propose a co-design approach:
to develop robot mechanisms and learning paradigms together, from the ground up, for human-centric tasks.

During my PhD, I thoroughly analysed of serial robots which makes the largest class of commercial robots. 

Such robots are referred to as nR mechanisms, wherein links are connected in series via 'n' revolute joints.
For instance, this images show 6R and a 7R robots, which have six and seven revolute joints respectively.
These robots ae the current dominating design in the industry and research.

A central question in robotics is inverse kinematics: for one target in space, how many joint configurations can reach it?
Let us consider a simplified case of a 2R robot where upto two IKS exist for a given target. This number explodes to 16 for 6R robots.

As these figures show, robotics lives in two spaces: the workspace, which describes where the robot’s end-effector can move, and the joint space, which describes the robot through its joint values.

This brings us to singularities, configurations where the robot loses motion capability and control becomes unreliable.

While singularities are bad, contingent on robot design, they allow us to distinguish between different IKS.
However the recent designs of robots, known as cuspidal robots, do not abide by this.

My PhD focused in particular on cuspidal robots, where the robot can switch from one inverse-kinematic solution to another without crossing a singularity.
This makes motion planning much harder, because robot postures are no longer cleanly separated. Cuspidality is therefore one source of unreliable execution that motivates my proposal.

Before my work, path-planning issues had mainly been studied for 3R cuspidal robots, and only isolated results existed for 6R robots.
This led to two main questions:
first, how can we determine whether a given 6R robot is cuspidal?
and second, what does cuspidality imply for the path planning of commercial robots?

To answer this, I combined geometric, algebraic, and numerical tools to build a practical framework for cuspidality analysis and classification.
One key result was that even small deviations from conventional industrial design often lead to cuspidal behaviour.
And once that happens, the robot admits specific path types that make motion planning much more subtle.
I also developed a framework for safe and reliable path planning of commercial 6R robots.


To move beyond single robot execution, my next investigation was along the line: how can I make programming collaborative robots intuitive, reliable, and transferable?

The control policy, a map from the robot’s current state to its next action is acquired from a human demonstration, and the challenge is to transfer this across robots.

In much of the current state of the art, transfer is handled through data-driven adaptation, morphology inference, or repeated tuning for each robot.
My approach is different.

First, we use stable dynamical systems to encode the demonstrated behaviour as robust control policies.
Second, we inject analytical kinematic properties directly into the learning model, so that execution remains faithful to the morphology of each robot.

The key idea is the following:
the policy can remain robot-agnostic,
but the execution must remain robot-specific.

This is what we call Kinematic Intelligence.

And this line of work culminated in our recent Science Robotics result on explainable cross-robot skill transfer.

Taken together, my PhD and postdoctoral work provide the two foundations of my CNRS project:
a global understanding of robot kinematics,
and the embedding of this understanding into learning and control.

In the following presentation, I will focus on how I address the two barriers of Reliable Execution and Skill Scalability, which structure the short-term and mid-term scientific goals of my project. To make the storyline easier to follow, each phase uses the same color code as the barrier it resolves.

The first phase of my proposal addresses the first barrier: reliable execution.

The motivation is straightforward.
Many modern commercial robots, including collaborative robots, are cuspidal.
At the same time, new robot morphologies are appearing very quickly.
So the analysis of cuspidal robots is no longer a niche question.
It is becoming essential for the future of human-robot interaction.

On the design side, the scientific lock is that kinematic analysis becomes drastically harder as dimension increases.
In simple words, this problem suffers from a curse of dimensionality.

My approach is to study the singularities and workspace structure of cuspidal robots systematically.
I want to classify robots through the topology of their singularities. 
To have global and non-stochastic perspective on the workspace, I will combine analytical and semi-analytical tools for fast and practical analysis.

In this step, I will use modern tools such as Conformal Geometric Algebra and Cylindrical Algebraic Decomposition.

This image shows an example of redundancy parameterization, instead of analyzing a full 7R robot all at once, I decompose it into substructures and study their singularities separately.
This gives a tractable route toward global analysis.

On the control side, the lock is different.
Existing policy-learning approaches work well when inverse-kinematic solutions are clearly separated by singularities, because then each solution can be associated with its own isolated policy.

But in cuspidal robots, this clean separation disappears.
Several solutions may coexist without singular separation, and the robot may face ambiguity in execution.

So the question becomes:
which control policy should the robot follow to reach a target?

My answer is to extend kinematic intelligence to cuspidal settings.
Instead of correcting the motion afterward, I want to inject kinematic structure into the policy from the start.
When several feasible solutions exist, the controller should identify, in real time, the one that best preserves the demonstrated intent.

And this must be done without creating unstable behaviours such as false attractors, oscillations, or dead zones.

So the objective of Phase 1 is the following:
to understand cuspidal robots globally,
and to use that understanding to build deterministic and explainable control policies.
This is the first concrete expression of my co-design philosophy.

Once reliable execution is secured, I turn to the second barrier: skill scalability.

Phase 1 addresses this for serial robots, but future robots will not all follow today’s architectures. As robot designs become increasingly diverse, skill transfer must also evolve beyond serial robots and remain valid across new morphologies and actuation principles.

This is especially important because the mechanical landscape itself is changing:
Conventional robots are precise, but rigid.
Soft robots are compliant, but they often lack precision and payload.
For human-robot interaction, especially in healthcare, we need a middle ground:
robots that are lightweight, mechanically compliant, and capable of variable stiffness.

This is why I am interested in novel joints and hybrid mechanisms.
For example, anti-parallelogram or X-joints are attractive because they mimic more natural joint motion and can exhibit positive co-activation:
as cables are tightened, stiffness increases.

On the design side, the scientific lock is that the kinematic analysis of such joints and hybrid robots is still largely missing.
My approach will be to study their singularities, cuspidal properties, and the effect of joint limits, and then identify which of these properties can be exploited for reliable control.

I also want to investigate hybrid serial-parallel mechanisms that combine natural rolling motion, low inertia, and better suitability for physical interaction.

Because these mechanisms often lead to complex nonlinear models, I will rely not only on classical analytical tools, but also on efficient numerical and semi-analytical methods.

On the control side, the scientific lock is the difficulty of transferring a learned skill from one robot to another when the target robot has fundamentally different kinematic characteristics.

My answer is to extend kinematic intelligence into a true skill-transfer framework.

The idea is that singularities and inverse-kinematic solutions form a kind of structural language of the robot.
By leveraging this language, I want to determine what should remain invariant during transfer, and how execution should adapt so that it remains safe, deterministic, and explainable across robot generations.

This is the phase where co-design becomes fully operational.
I do not only want robots that can learn safely.
I want robots whose very mechanics are designed to make safe and explainable learning possible.
